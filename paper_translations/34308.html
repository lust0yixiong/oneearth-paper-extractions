Introduction Despite decades of research, the mechanisms and environmental cues guiding long-distance migratory birds continue to be a subject of ongoing scientific inquiry (Hagstrum, 2023; Lohmann et al., 2022; Mouritsen, 2018; Wallraff, 2004). This is particularly true for the pro­ cess of true navigation, where birds utilise local information to navigate towards distant targets (Holland, 2014). Birds use a combination of global cues such as celestial cues, visual landmarks, olfactory informa­ tion, infrasound, and Earth's magnetic field (Deutschlander and Beason, 2014; Gagliardo and Bingman, 2024; Hagstrum, 2000; Wallraff, 2005). However, it is still poorly understood how they integrate these cues within a complex and dynamic environment, translating them into effective navigational strategies and, ultimately, into movement de­ cisions. This paper employs a new data-driven analysis approach to study navigational cue integration. We combined long-term tracking data from migratory birds with environmental data and used a data mining method (hierarchical clustering), to identify how patterns in navigational behaviour are related to the environmental conditions that birds experienced on their journeys. Although data-driven methods have been used to analyse animal movement (Dodge et al., 2014; Eikelboom et al., 2020; Wan et al., 2023), this is the first application of hierarchical clustering to examine how multiple cues might jointly shape real-time avian navigation. Within the map and compass framework, the Earth's magnetic field has been proposed as a potential source of both map and compass information (Lohmann et al., 2022). Nonetheless, researchers continue to debate whether geomagnetic cues alone are sufficient for the “map” sense or whether additional mechanisms—such as olfactory navigation (Bonadonna and Gagliardo, 2021; Gagliardo et al., 2021; Wallraff, 2004; Wallraff, 2005), infrasound (Hagstrum, 2000; Hagstrum, 2013; Patrick et al., 2021), or celestial compasses (Alerstam et al., 2001; Muheim et al., 2006)—are essential. The Earth's magnetic field is a complex and dynamic phenomenon, with the core field providing its foundation, the crustal field adding regional variations, and the external field, influenced by the solar wind, introducing dynamic fluctuations (Lanza and Meloni, 2006). An animal on the surface of the Earth or in the atmosphere experiences the cu­ mulative effect of the core, crustal, and external fields—collectively referred to as the geomagnetic field. This geomagnetic field provides a global reference frame characterised by three primary variables: in­ tensity (the strength of the geomagnetic field), inclination (the angle between the Earth's surface and the geomagnetic field lines), and declination (the angle between geomagnetic north and true north) (Fig. 1).

Several theoretical models have been proposed to explain how birds might use the geomagnetic field for compass orientation. Two promi­ nent models are the geomagnetic loxodrome and the magnetoclinic route. A geomagnetic loxodrome is a path of travel with a constant bearing relative to the geomagnetic north. This strategy is efficient for maintaining a steady course over long distances (Alerstam et al., 2001). A magnetoclinic route involves maintaining a constant apparent angle of geomagnetic inclination relative to the geomagnetic field. This is believed to help birds navigate by keeping a consistent orientation (Kiepenheuer, 1984). Compass orientation is challenging because it can be disrupted by local environmental factors, particularly wind. Although favourable tailwinds can boost flight efficiency (Alerstam, 2011; Grönroos et al., 2012) and some birds time their departures accordingly (Åkesson and Hedenström, 2000; Kölzsch et al., 2016; Rüppel et al., 2023; Sjöberg et al., 2015), reliance on a single compass leaves birds vulnerable to drift (Chapman et al., 2011). To compensate, they must recalibrate their orientation by referencing a “map” sense, which may be geomagnetic, olfactory, or even infrasound-based (Chernetsov et al., 2008; Gagliardo et al., 2011; Hagstrum, 2000; Holland, 2014). While the map sense's exact mechanismremainscontentious(Bonadonna andGagliardo, 2021; Hagstrum, 2023; Wallraff, 2004; Wiltschko and Wiltschko, 2017), geomagnetic gradients—such as latitudinal variations in inclination and intensity—are hypothesised to provide birds with a positional frame­ work (Dennis et al., 2007; Kishkinev et al., 2013; Lohmann et al., 2022; Wiltschko and Wiltschko, 2023). For a more detailed discussion of these debates, see the reviews by Able (2001) and Demšar et al. (2025).

Additionally, localised geomagnetic anomalies, caused by geological features, might serve as navigational landmarks or signposts to confirm position and adjust course (Heyers et al., 2017; Lohmann, 1991; Wiltschko and Wiltschko, 2006). Traditional investigations into avian navigation mechanisms have relied primarily on laboratory experiments and displacement studies, where individual birds are exposed to altered navigational cues to observe changes in their migration/movement behaviour (Chernetsov et al., 2008; Muheim et al., 2002; Wiltschko et al., 2006). While these controlled settings provide insights into the sensory and cognitive ca­ pabilities of birds, they offer a limited understanding of the real-time decision-making processes involved in long-distance migration within a complex and dynamic natural environment (Deutschlander and Bea­ son, 2014). Furthermore, most studies focus on single navigational mechanisms, overlooking the possibility of a dynamic, multi-modal navigational system where birds flexibly switch between cues in response to prevailing conditions (Mouritsen et al., 2016). Given the limitations of traditional approaches, there has been a growing interest in data-driven methods, such as data mining, to study avian movement. The increasing availability of high-resolution tracking data, combined with global-scale environmental datasets, has opened new opportunities to observe and analyse the behaviour of migratory birds during their journeys (Farley et al., 2018; Kays et al., 2022; Wil­ liams et al., 2020). Unlike conventional, hypothesis-driven research, which begins with a predefined question and collects data specifically to test it, data-driven approaches work in reverse, starting with large datasets to detect patterns, which then inform new research questions (Demšar et al., 2025). This shift from deductive to exploratory analysis enables researchers to uncover previously unrecognised relationships, forming abductive hypotheses based on emergent patterns rather than predefined assumptions (Miller and Goodchild, 2015). Recent studies have begun to harness this potential, employing sta­ tistical movement models and machine learning techniques to investi­ gate navigational strategies in migratory birds (Nicol et al., 2024; Wang, 2019).

Methods and data 3.1. GPS tracking data We used high-resolution GPS tracking data from 117 adult greater white-fronted geese (Anser albifrons), ensuring that no two geese belonged to the same flock. These data, totalling over 6 million data points, were collected over a decade (2014–2024; see Data Availability section). Tracking data preprocessing Initial preprocessing involved the removal of data points with insufficient satellite fixes (<4) to ensure accurate geolocation. Outlier detection methods were employed to exclude points with anomalous locations and those exhibiting unrealistic speeds exceeding the known flight capabilities of the species, potentially indicative of GPS error (Hebblewhite and Haydon, 2010). To focus our analysis on the migra­ tory behaviour of the North Sea population of greater white-fronted geese, we excluded individuals identified as having changed their pop­ ulation affiliation (Kölzsch et al., 2019). Finally, we removed tracks where geese did not complete the full migration between breeding and wintering sites, as these incomplete journeys could bias our analysis of migratory behaviour. To isolate active migratory flight data from periods of foraging, moulting, or nesting, we implemented a multi-step filtering process. First, we identified stopover sites as locations where individuals stayed within a 30 km radius for more than 48 h (van Wijk et al., 2012). Similarly, nesting and moulting sites were defined as areas where geese stayed for more than 10 days within 2 km and more than 21 days within 30 km, respectively (Kölzsch et al., 2019). Data points within these areas were excluded. Next, we applied speed filters, removing data points with GPS-measured speeds below 10 m/s (Kölzsch et al., 2016) and calcu­ lated speeds (displacement/time) below 1.7 m/s (Zein et al., 2021), thereby retaining only data points likely representing active flight dur­ ing migration. To examine seasonal and diurnal variations in migratory behaviour, migration periods were defined as spring (1March – 1 June) and autumn (15 August – 15 November) (Kölzsch et al., 2019).

The pixel containing each GPS point was located, and the values at the four corners and the centre were extracted. Distance-weighted interpolation was then applied to both raster datasets using these five reference points and inverse distance weights based on great circle distances. The two resulting values were linearly interpolated, weighted by the time difference between raster and GPS timestamps (Fig. 2). 3.5. Cluster analysis We applied agglomerative hierarchical cluster analysis to GPS fixes, using the navigational cues from Section 3.4 as cluster attributes, excluding spatial coordinates. This method aimed to reveal times and locations where bird movements correspond with similar navigational cues. We then hypothesised that by mapping and interpreting these clusters, we would beable to identify segments ofmigratory flight where different navigational environmental cues are being used. Agglomerative hierarchical clustering (AHC) was chosen for its suitability for complex ecological data, as it does not require a pre­ defined number of clusters and can effectively handle nested groupings often observed in natural systems (Murtagh and Contreras, 2017; Wan et al., 2023). Using Ward's linkage, we minimised within-cluster vari­ ance while maximising between-cluster variance, facilitating the iden­ tification of discrete behavioural modes (Murtagh and Legendre, 2014). Input data were Z-score standardised to ensure equal feature weighting, with outliers beyond ±5 standard deviations removed. Euclidean dis­ tance was used to measure dissimilarity between data points. The analysis was conducted independently for spring daytime, spring nighttime, autumn daytime, and autumn nighttime datasets to capture variations under different conditions. Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering (AHC) generates a dendro­ gram, a tree-like diagram that visualises how individual data points or small clusters merge into larger clusters at each step. Cluster determi­ nation involves analysing this dendrogram to decide where to cut it, which means selecting a level in the hierarchy where the clusters are defined. This step is critical because the number of clusters directly impacts the interpretation and biological relevance of the analysis. One common approach to determine the cut point is to use cluster validation indices such as the Silhouette index (Rousseeuw, 1987) and the Calinski- Harabasz index (Caliński and Harabasz, 1974). We chose to complement the above validation indices with a strategy that involved cutting the dendrogram at a height where the largest resulting cluster contained no more than 25 % of the total data points.

这确保了更加平衡和细粒度的聚类解决方案，防止任何单个聚类主导分析并揭示潜在的重要但较小的子组（Hennig，2007；Stanberry 等人，2003）。为了解释聚类的行为含义，我们计算了聚类过程中未包含的四个运动参数：运动速度、转向角度以及与下一个中途停留地和最终目的地的直接路径的角度偏差（表1；图6）。这种两步方法首先根据特定属性定义聚类，然后使用其他行为指标进行解释，确保初始聚类标准的解释保持公正，从而在环境条件和行为反应之间建立更清晰的联系（Barraquand 和 Benhamou，2008 年；Mose 和 Western，2015 年；Wan 等人，2023 年）。运动速度和转动角度是运动生态学中广泛使用的指标（Turchin，1998）。转弯角度揭示了飞行路径曲折度的变化（Estevez 和 Christman，2006），而运动速度则量化了迁徙飞行期间的步速（Demšar 等，2015）。为了避免瞬时测量结果不准确，这些指标是使用 R 中的移动包在连续 GPS 点之间计算的（Kranstauber 等人，2018）。另一方面，角度偏差量化了鸟类将其航向与到达迁徙目标的最短路径对齐的程度，从而提供了对导航准确性的洞察。我们将直接路径的角度偏差定义为给定 GPS 点的鸟的航向与其最终目的地 (σD) 或其下一个中途停留区域中心 (σS) 的方位之间的角度差。我们进一步检查了集群成员资格，以评估所识别的模式是否在人群中具有普遍性或受特定个体的影响。

Discussion Our findings indicate that greater white-fronted geese navigate in a flexible and context-dependent manner, integrating multiple cues rather than relying on a single strategy. By applying hierarchical clustering to long-term, high-resolution tracking data, we uncovered patterns that remain hidden when examining cues independently. A key observation was the frequent occurrence of “turning point” clusters—characterised by notable changes in apparent inclination (ΔI') and geomagnetic heading (Δψm)—during spring migrations, a season in which resource constraints and migration goals may necessitate more complex navigational adjustments (Kölzsch et al., 2016). In autumn, these turning points were much less common, as geese travelled more directly, reflecting different environmental conditions and a reduced need for mid-flight course changes. Notably, the eastwarddivergence observed in spring tracks likely reflects increased foraging efforts aimed at resource accumulation for optimal breeding, whereas autumn migrations are more direct due to the abundance of food resources at both departure and arrival (Kölzsch et al., 2016; van Wijk et al., 2012). Diurnal cycles added another layer of complexity, with nighttime flights more closely aligned with wind direction and prioritising intermediate stopovers over distant destinations.

Future studies could also incorporate additional navigational inputs, such as solar and celestial navigation, olfaction, polarised light, detailed visual landmarks and social cues, to better capture the multi­ sensory integration involved in avian navigation. Lastly, integrating GPS tracking data with physiological or neurobiological measurements would allow for a more nuanced understanding of the internal processes guiding avian migration, revealing how cognitive states influence decision-making during these long journeys. 6. Conclusion This data mining investigation provides novel insights into the realtime decision-making processes involved in long-distance migration, highlighting the importance of environmental context for migratory strategies. By integrating high-resolution tracking data with diverse environmental cues, our study revealed a dynamic interplay between geomagnetic, wind, and visual information in shaping the migratory strategies of greater white-fronted geese. Our study provides empirical support for the use of geomagnetic loxodrome and magnetoclinic routes in free-flying birds within their natural environment. Under favourable tailwinds, geese employ flexible strategies that adapt to environmental conditions, diurnal cycles, and likely flock-specific characteristics, facilitating efficient flights either towards stopovers or simultaneously aligning with stopovers and final destinations. Wind emerged as a crit­ ical factor in geese's navigation, with tailwinds enabling efficient, energy-saving travel and crosswinds necessitating compass recalibration to minimise drift. These findings, along with the observed integration of visual cues with geomagnetic information, underscore the complexity and adaptability of avian navigation, extending beyond the traditional singular map-and-compass model.
